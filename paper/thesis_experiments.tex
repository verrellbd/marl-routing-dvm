\section{Experiments}

% NOTE: this chapter describes the configuration that produced
% results/final_ns3_grid.json (378 ns-3 simulations, 0 failures, dirs results/ns3f_*).
% Launchers: train_matched_hparams.sh (training), run_ns3_final.sh (evaluation).

This section describes how the multi-agent policy is trained, how it is evaluated
zero-shot on networks it has never seen, and how it is compared against OSPF,
ECMP, and a centralized single-agent controller.

The central design decision is that \emph{training and evaluation use disjoint
networks}. One policy is trained on seventeen SNDlib topologies with synthetic
gravity-model traffic and is then evaluated, without any retraining or
fine-tuning, on three backbones with real measured demand. This is a stricter
test than the per-topology protocol usual in the literature, where a policy is
trained and tested on the same graph, and it is what allows the resulting policy
to be described as topology-agnostic.

\subsection{Computing Environment and Reproducibility}
\label{sec:setup-compute}

All experiments were conducted on a single Linux server (Rocky 9) with an AMD
EPYC 7543 processor ($128$ hardware threads) and $512$\,GB of RAM. Training and
evaluation are CPU-only; no GPU is used. Runs are parallelised across cores
rather than within them, with nine concurrent jobs each pinned to a single thread
via \texttt{OMP\_NUM\_THREADS=1}, \texttt{MKL\_NUM\_THREADS} and
\texttt{OPENBLAS\_NUM\_THREADS}. Thread pinning is necessary rather than
cosmetic: uncapped, each PyTorch process spawns one thread per core and the
resulting oversubscription exhausts memory.

The software stack is Python 3.9, ns-3.42, PyTorch 2.6.0, PyTorch Geometric
2.6.1, NetworkX, and Stable-Baselines3 2.7.1. The pipeline is implemented as a
set of versioned Python modules and shell launchers rather than a notebook, so
that every reported figure traces to a recorded command: training is driven by
\texttt{train\_matched\_hparams.sh} and packet-level evaluation by
\texttt{run\_ns3\_final.sh}. Traffic matrices are generated from fixed seeds, and
trained policies, routing tables, and per-simulation measurements are all
persisted alongside the code.

SNDlib does not ship measured dynamic traffic for most of these instances. Since
the training set exists to supply \emph{structural} diversity rather than
realistic demand, traffic for it is synthesised with the TMgen library using the
modulated-gravity model \cite{internet-traffic}, which represents inter-node
traffic by analogy with Newton's law of gravitation and is the standard
generator for this purpose. Three independent spatial patterns are drawn per
topology and each is swept over five volume scales
$\{0.6, 0.8, 1.0, 1.2, 1.5\}$, where a pattern is normalised so that scale $1.0$
places the OSPF bottleneck at exactly $100\%$. Each topology therefore
contributes fifteen matrices spanning both congestion regimes. Matrices are
truncated to their $500$ largest demands to keep episodes tractable.

The reservation of measured data for testing is deliberate: demand realism
matters least where the policy is fitted and most where it is judged.

\paragraph{Demand scaling.}
\label{sec:setup-scaling}
Measured demand on all three backbones is light relative to installed capacity,
so shortest-path routing is trivially optimal at the native magnitude and no
routing method can distinguish itself. Following standard practice in traffic
engineering studies, we retain the measured spatial and temporal \emph{structure}
of the matrices and scale only their overall \emph{magnitude} by a factor
$z\in\mathcal{Z}$ to set the operating point. Nothing in the demand pattern is
synthetic; $z$ is a single scalar applied uniformly to every entry of a measured
matrix.

Two scale sets are recorded for Abilene because a correctly costed OSPF is never
overloaded there at $\{8,12,16\}$: with the single $2.48$\,Gbps link priced at
four times a $9.92$\,Gbps link, OSPF routes around it and the network absorbs the
demand. Exercising the overload regime on Abilene therefore requires the higher
set $\{16,22,28\}$. Germany50 has the converse problem, every measured matrix
overloading OSPF at $\{35,50,65\}$, so its feasible regime is measured separately
at $\{15,20,25,30\}$. Consequently the Abilene rows of the analytical and
packet-level tables describe the same policies at different operating points and
must not be read across.

\paragraph{Train/test separation.}
Separation here is by \emph{network}, not merely by time: no evaluation topology
and no measured matrix is seen during training. Within each evaluation network the
demand time series is additionally split temporally, the last $30\%$ of the
timeline forming the test interval, with candidate timestamps sampled evenly
across it. A temporal rather than random split is used deliberately: matrices five
minutes apart are near duplicates, and random interleaving would make the retained
matrices mutual near-copies.

\subsection{Methods Compared}
\label{sec:setup-methods}

Four routing methods are evaluated on identical inputs, together with the two greedy references of Section~\ref{sec:res-greedy}.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{OSPF.} Shortest-path routing under the standard OSPF cost
    metric, $\text{cost}(e) = \text{refBW}/\text{cap}(e)$, with the reference
    bandwidth set to the fastest link in the network. This is the configuration
    deployed in practice, and it is materially stronger than a hop-count
    baseline on any network with heterogeneous capacities.
    \item \textbf{ECMP.} Equal-cost multipath over the paths that the same cost
    metric makes tied. Because the tie is on \emph{cost} rather than hop count,
    ECMP inherits OSPF's capacity awareness. Splitting is at \emph{flow}
    granularity: each demand is assigned one of its tied paths, as a router
    assigns by hashing the flow's header, rather than divided fractionally across
    all of them. The same assignment is used for the analytical and the
    packet-level results, so ECMP denotes one policy throughout.
    \item \textbf{Single-agent GNN.} A centralized policy with full-network
    observability, trained with PPO, which selects one of $k=3$ candidate paths
    for each demand in turn.
    \item \textbf{MARL.} An agent at each node observes local link state and
    chooses the next hop, so a route is constructed hop by hop rather than
    selected whole. The reported policy uses hidden width $32$, selected on the
    training topologies as described in Section~\ref{sec:setup-hyper}.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Capacity-aware path construction.} All learned methods choose among
paths derived from the same OSPF cost metric as the baselines: the single-agent
controller's $k=3$ candidates are cost-shortest rather than hop-shortest, and the
MARL agents' progress test and detour limit are expressed in cost units rather
than hops. This matters on networks with heterogeneous capacities. Under a
hop-based construction, every candidate path for a given demand may traverse the
one slow link, leaving the policy no capacity-avoiding route to select; on
Abilene this affected $112$ of $396$ candidate paths, a figure the cost-based
construction reduces to $50$. Without this alignment the learned methods would be
compared against a capacity-aware baseline while themselves being capacity-blind.

\paragraph{Controlled comparison.} The controlled variable is the path, and only
the path. All four methods are installed in ns-3 through the identical
static-route mechanism, over the same topology, the same demand matrix, the same
flow set, and the same simulator configuration; the routing tables are the sole
difference between runs. Where a flow cap is applied (top $200$ demands by rate
on Germany50, needed to keep packet-level simulation tractable), the cap is
computed once per matrix and the resulting flow set is used identically by every
method.

\subsection{Learner Configuration and Budget Matching}
\label{sec:setup-hyper}

Table~\ref{tab:hyper} records the learner settings. Every optimiser
hyperparameter is identical between the two architectures, so a difference in
outcome is attributable to decentralisation rather than to tuning.

The direction in which they were equalised is deliberate. The centralized
controller is the \emph{baseline}, and reducing a baseline's optimisation effort
to match one's own method would amount to handicapping it. Its settings are
therefore Stable-Baselines3's defaults, left untouched, and the MAPPO
implementation was moved onto them: the discount was raised from $0.99$ to
$0.995$, epochs per update from $6$ to $10$, and the minibatch reduced from $512$
to $256$, with the rollout buffer set to $2048$ so that both methods perform the
same $732$ updates.

\paragraph{Parameter counts are not matched, and cannot usefully be.} Equal hidden
width does not imply equal capacity across two structurally different networks.
The centralized policy carries $268{,}420$ trainable parameters, of which
$251{,}520$ sit in the feature extractor that encodes the padded arc-state vector;
the reported decentralized policy carries $17{,}570$. The centralized controller
therefore has fifteen times the capacity of the policy it is compared against, and any advantage the decentralized method
shows is obtained with an order of magnitude fewer parameters. Matching the two
on parameter count would require distorting one architecture, so the disparity is
reported rather than engineered away.

\paragraph{Width selection.} The reported width was chosen on the \emph{training}
topologies, not on the evaluation backbones. Both widths were scored on held-out
TMgen matrices drawn from the seventeen training instances under a generator seed
disjoint from training (\texttt{select\_width.py}), giving a mean reduction in
bottleneck load against OSPF of $23.5 \pm 1.4$ percentage points at width $32$
and $19.2 \pm 1.6$ at width $64$, with no overlap between the two arms' per-seed
ranges. Width $32$ is therefore adopted and $h{=}64$ is not reported. Selecting on
test-backbone performance instead would have compromised the zero-shot claim those
three networks exist to support; that the same ordering holds there is treated as
confirmation, not as the basis of the choice.

\paragraph{Reward.} Environment settings are shared: delay penalty
$\beta = 0.5$, detour allowance $\sigma = 2$ and maximum stretch
$\sigma_{\max} = 4$ cost units, reward normalisation by the episode's OSPF
bottleneck, and a $500$-demand cap on training matrices, for both architectures
and every network.

The reward function itself is also identical, which required a change. The
centralized environment originally charged $\beta$ per \emph{extra hop} relative
to the shortest candidate and normalised that penalty together with the
congestion term, whereas the decentralized environment charges a flat $\beta$ per
\emph{detour hop} --- a hop that does not reduce cost-distance to the destination
--- and leaves it unnormalised. Both arms now use the latter. Per demand the two
are then exactly equal: because arc loads only increase, the decentralized
environment's per-hop congestion increments telescope to the same
$\max$-utilisation difference the centralized environment charges once for the
whole path, and that difference does not depend on the order in which a path's
arcs are added. Section~\ref{sec:res-reward} reports what the change was worth;
the original form is retained as an ablation.

What cannot be equalised, and is not, is how that reward is \emph{delivered}. The
decentralized policy receives it spread across the hops of a demand and the
centralized policy as a single terminal quantity, so under a common discount the
two face different value targets and different effective horizons. That is a
property of the action granularity being compared, not a residual mismatch.

\paragraph{An unmatchable axis.} One budget dimension cannot be equalised, and is
reported rather than concealed. A single step of the centralized environment
routes an entire demand, whereas a single MARL step advances one hop. At a matched
$1.5$M environment steps the centralized controller therefore routes roughly six
times as many demands, since paths on these networks average about six hops. The
budget is matched on environment steps, the conventional unit, but not on routing
work, and the mismatch favours the centralized method. Where the decentralized
policy nonetheless wins, under-training of the baseline cannot be the explanation.

\subsection{Congestion Regimes}
\label{sec:setup-regimes}

Results are reported separately for two congestion regimes, because the routing
problem is qualitatively different in each: when demand fits, OSPF is already
close to optimal and the achievable gain is small; when it does not, path choice
determines how much traffic is lost.

Let $G=(V,E)$ be the network with arc capacities $c_e$ (Mb/s), and let
$\mathcal{D}=\{(s,d)\}$ be the set of source--destination pairs with demands
$r_{sd}$. A routing $\pi$ assigns each pair a path $P^{\pi}_{sd}$, giving arc
loads and \emph{offered} utilisations
\begin{equation}
  \ell_e(\pi) \;=\; \sum_{(s,d)\in\mathcal{D}} r_{sd}\,
  \mathbb{1}\!\left[e \in P^{\pi}_{sd}\right],
  \qquad
  u^{\mathrm{off}}_e(\pi) \;=\; 100\cdot\frac{\ell_e(\pi)}{c_e}\quad[\%],
  \label{eq:util}
\end{equation}
and the congestion metric is the maximum offered link utilisation
\begin{equation}
  U^{\mathrm{off}}(\pi) \;=\; \max_{e\in E} u^{\mathrm{off}}_e(\pi).
  \label{eq:maxutil}
\end{equation}
Quantity~\eqref{eq:maxutil} is \emph{uncapped}: it is computed from demands and
may exceed $100\%$, which is precisely what makes ``how far past capacity a
routing pushes the network'' measurable. It is distinct from the utilisation
measured inside ns-3, written $U^{\mathrm{ns\text{-}3}}$, which counts bytes
actually carried per device and therefore saturates at $100\%$ by construction:
offered load beyond capacity appears as packet loss, not as utilisation above
line rate. Both are reported, and they are never conflated.

Test matrices are stratified by the offered utilisation that \emph{OSPF} would
incur, $U^{\mathrm{off}}_{\mathrm{OSPF}}$:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{regime}(r) \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    \textbf{overload}, & U^{\mathrm{off}}_{\mathrm{OSPF}}(r) \;\ge\; 100\%,\\[2pt]
    \textbf{feasible}, & U^{\mathrm{off}}_{\mathrm{OSPF}}(r) \;<\; 100\%.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:regime}
\end{equation}
The label is a property of the traffic matrix under the baseline alone. It is
assigned before any learned policy is evaluated and does not depend on the
learned routing, so the stratification cannot favour the proposed method. From
the $18$ candidate matrices per network ($6$ per demand scale) we retain the
three most congested overloaded matrices and the three most stressed feasible
matrices, that is, the hardest instance of each regime rather than an average
one. With three seeds this gives nine measurements per learned arm, per network
and regime.

\subsection{Packet-Level Simulation}
\label{sec:setup-ns3}

Every reported loss, delay, and utilisation figure is measured in ns-3 at packet
level, not by the analytical model used to shape the training reward. Each link
is a \texttt{PointToPointNetDevice} whose transmit queue is the default DropTail
queue of $100$ packets; no active queue management, shaping, or congestion
control is configured, so reported loss is pure buffer overflow. Traffic is
one-way UDP: each demand is a constant-rate \texttt{OnOff} application with a
$1400$-byte payload, so that loss and delay are attributable to the routing
decision and not to a transport-layer feedback loop. Propagation delays are
installed at nanosecond resolution, which matters on Germany50 where several
links are below $1$\,ms and would otherwise truncate to zero.

Each simulation covers $8$\,s of simulated time with all flows active over the
window $[2\,\mathrm{s},\,9\,\mathrm{s}]$. Because the byte counters run over the
full $8$\,s window while flows are active for $7$\,s, all utilisation figures are
divided by the exact, uniform factor $7/8$ to recover true utilisation; the
correction is identical for every method and every run. Demand rates and link
capacities are both divided by a common scale factor of $20$, which leaves every
utilisation, loss, and delay ratio invariant while reducing packet count enough to
make the full evaluation feasible in wall-clock time. The complete grid comprises
$378$ simulations, all of which completed successfully.

A consequence of installing exact per-flow routes should be stated: these are
source-specific paths, expressible in a deployed network through MPLS or segment
routing but not through plain destination-based IP forwarding. The mechanism is a
faithful way to measure what a learned routing achieves, not a claim that it
drops into an existing IP control plane unchanged.

\subsection{Performance Metrics}
\label{sec:setup-metrics}

Four quantities are reported. The first is analytical; the remaining three are
measured in ns-3.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Maximum offered load}, Equation~\eqref{eq:maxutil}. The training
    objective, and the only quantity that can express how far past capacity a
    routing pushes its worst link.
  \item \textbf{Maximum link utilisation.} The highest per-device utilisation
    observed in simulation, saturating at $100\%$. Informative in the feasible
    regime, where it measures the headroom a routing leaves.
  \item \textbf{Packet loss.} The proportion of dropped packets, as a percentage.
    The decisive metric in the overload regime, where every method saturates its
    bottleneck and the difference between them is what happens to the excess.
  \item \textbf{Delay.} Mean end-to-end packet transmission time, in
    milliseconds.
\end{enumerate}

Delay must be read jointly with loss and never in isolation. Dropped packets
contribute no delay sample, so a routing that loses more traffic can report a
lower mean delay purely as an artefact of survivorship. We therefore report loss
and delay side by side and treat a delay reduction as meaningful only when loss is
equal or lower.

Results for the learned methods are summarised as mean $\pm$ standard deviation
across the three model seeds, each seed contributing its own mean over the
retained matrices. Deviations are quoted for those arms only. OSPF is
deterministic given a matrix and has nothing to vary over. ECMP does make a
choice --- when several paths tie on cost, one is selected per flow, as a real
router selects by hashing the flow's header --- and we reproduce that with a
seeded draw, but the resulting spread measures tie-breaking rather than training
and is not commensurable with a spread over model seeds. ECMP is therefore
reported as the mean over three hash seeds, without a deviation, so that the
$\pm$ symbol carries one meaning throughout. The tie-breaking spread is not
negligible and is worth recording separately: on GÉANT the hash choice alone
moves bottleneck offered load between $57.5\%$ and $63.1\%$, and on Germany50
between $198.9\%$ and $219.4\%$, which is variation a deployed network would
actually experience between routers.

Flow-level assignment is the conservative choice for ECMP but not the flattering
one. An idealised variant that divided each demand fractionally across all its
tied paths --- unimplementable at flow granularity, and approximated in practice
only by per-packet spraying at the cost of reordering --- would be considerably
stronger, reaching $106.1\%$ rather than $119.2\%$ on GÉANT and $181.5\%$ rather
than $211.9\%$ on Germany50. The flow-level figure is reported because it is the
policy actually installed in the simulator and the one operators run; the fluid
figure is quoted here so that the gap between them is on record.

No significance test is reported across seeds: $n=3$ is far too small to support
one, and a $p$-value over three points would be misleading. Seed variation is
reported descriptively, and a result is described as robust only when its sign is
consistent across all three seeds.
