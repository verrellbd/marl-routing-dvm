\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}
The study evaluated results on the Abilene, GÉANT, and Germany50 topologies using real measured operator traffic. None of these topologies were included in the policy's training data. Under overload conditions, the proposed approach reduces packet loss compared to OSPF by $11.2$ percentage points on GÉANT and $18.4$ on Germany50. In the feasible regime, it maintains GÉANT's bottleneck utilization at $66.7\%$, whereas OSPF results in $97.3\%$, with no increase in loss or delay. On Abilene, the approach does not outperform OSPF. These findings indicate that a single MARL policy can adapt to previously unseen networks and provide improvements on several topologies. Results are reported as mean $\pm$ standard deviation \emph{across model seeds}, each seed contributing its own mean over the retained matrices.

\label{sec:res-util}

load of Equation~\eqref{eq:maxutil}, on the held-out networks. Recall from
Section~\ref{sec:setup-regimes} that this quantity is uncapped and may exceed
$100\%$, denoting demand no queueing discipline can serve; its consequence is
quantified in Section~\ref{sec:res-qos}.

Both regimes are reported for all three networks. Doing so requires two demand
scales per network rather than one, because no single scale exercises both regimes
everywhere: a correctly costed OSPF is never overloaded on Abilene below a scale of
about $16$, and never feasible on Germany50 above about $30$
(Section~\ref{sec:setup-scaling}). Each cell is therefore measured at the scale set
that produces it, and cells are comparable down columns but not across regimes
within a network.

Every learned arm improves on OSPF in every cell but one, and the size of the
improvement tracks network scale. On GÉANT, MARL at $h{=}32$ moves the bottleneck
from $145.2\%$ to $96.6\%$, restoring feasibility, while the centralized policy
reaches only $133.7\%$ and remains infeasible. On the $50$-node Germany50 the gap
is wider still: MARL reduces a $249.2\%$ bottleneck to $117.0\%$ and the
centralized policy only to $176.3\%$. Abilene reverses the ordering. There the
centralized controller is the best arm in both regimes, at $116.3\%$ against
OSPF's $129.3\%$ under overload and $54.2\%$ against $57.2\%$ when feasible,
while MARL at $h{=}64$ is the single cell in which any learned method is worse
than OSPF ($130.3\%$ against $129.3\%$).

Three observations qualify the table. First, ECMP is a materially stronger
baseline than shortest-path OSPF wherever the topology offers equal-cost
alternatives: it reaches $119.2\%$ on GÉANT and $211.9\%$ on Germany50, so on
GÉANT the centralized policy is beaten not only by MARL but by a mechanism
already deployed in every operator network. On Abilene, whose fifteen links leave
few ties to split over, ECMP is indistinguishable from OSPF ($129.2\%$ against
$129.3\%$) and every learned arm but one beats both.

Second, the centralized policy's Germany50 dispersion --- $\pm 51.0$ percentage
points under overload, against $\pm 3.5$ for MARL at the same width and under
identical hyperparameters and reward --- is an order of magnitude larger than any
other entry in the table. This is examined in Section~\ref{sec:res-stability}.

Third, the objective and the packet-level measurement rank the Germany50 feasible
cell differently. Here MARL holds the bottleneck at $40.9\%$ against the
centralized policy's $52.3\%$; Section~\ref{sec:res-qos} shows the centralized
policy nonetheless delivering the better service in that cell, because MARL's
additional headroom is obtained at a cost in loss the surrogate does not model.
The disagreement is the clearest illustration of why the packet-level evaluation
is necessary rather than confirmatory.

\subsection{Packet-Level Quality of Service}
\label{sec:res-qos}

possible to lower a bottleneck metric and deliver worse service by lengthening
paths until propagation delay dominates. To rule this out, the exact per-flow
routes produced by each policy were installed in ns-3 and driven with the same
held-out traffic under the configuration of Section~\ref{sec:setup-ns3}.
Table~\ref{tab:qos} reports the resulting measurements over $378$ simulations.

\paragraph{Overload.} On GÉANT, MARL at $h{=}32$ reduces loss from $14.13\%$ to
$2.92\%$, a factor of nearly five and a margin of $11.2$ percentage points, while
holding delay at $18.1$\,ms against OSPF's $21.4$\,ms. The centralized controller
reaches $11.38\%$, an improvement on OSPF but one that ECMP already exceeds at
$6.82\%$. The delay result is the
more informative: the learned policy uses longer paths, so a naive expectation is
higher delay, and that it falls instead shows the dominant contribution under
OSPF was queueing at the saturated bottleneck rather than propagation. On
Germany50 the $h{=}64$ policy reduces loss from $27.29\%$ to $8.88\%$ and
simultaneously records the lowest delay in the cell, $15.1$\,ms against
$19.7$\,ms. The operational reading is direct: a link dropping one packet in
four, at which interactive and real-time traffic is unusable, is brought to a
loss rate an order of magnitude lower by changing route selection alone, with no
additional capacity provisioned.

The objective and the packet-level outcome agree throughout this regime.
Table~\ref{tab:offered-util} showed the centralized policy failing to restore
feasibility on GÉANT at $133.9\%$; Table~\ref{tab:qos} shows it correspondingly
retaining $11.38\%$ loss, closer to OSPF's than to MARL's. Policies that restore
feasibility deliver service, and policies that do not, do not.

Abilene inverts the ranking. There the centralized controller records the lowest
loss in the cell, $13.41\%$ against OSPF's $14.35\%$ and MARL's $15.41\%$, and is
the only learned arm to improve on both baselines. Its advantage is bought with
delay ($127.8$\,ms against MARL's $108.8$), so the two learned methods trade
rather than dominate.

\paragraph{Feasible.} The GÉANT feasible cell is the cleanest result in the
study: MARL at $h{=}32$ holds the bottleneck at $66.7\%$ against OSPF's $97.3\%$
while \emph{improving} both loss ($0.12\%$ against $0.16\%$) and delay
($8.3$\,ms against $9.5$\,ms). Thirty percentage points of headroom are freed at
no service cost, which is the margin an operator needs to absorb a link failure
or a traffic surge without re-engineering.

Germany50 behaves differently and the difference must not be averaged away. Both
MARL widths free substantially more headroom than OSPF, $70.8\%$ and $61.9\%$
against $95.6\%$, but they pay for it in loss: $1.02\%$ and $2.44\%$ against
OSPF's $0.03\%$. The centralized policy wins this cell outright, matching OSPF's
$0.03\%$ loss at $1.9$\,ms delay while holding the bottleneck $34.1$ points lower
at $61.5\%$ --- more headroom than either MARL width, at none of their loss cost.
Where the traffic already fits, the whole-path selection of the centralized
formulation extracts headroom without disturbing service, and the hop-by-hop MARL
formulation does not.

\subsection{Seed Stability and the Architectural Comparison}
\label{sec:res-stability}

Because all four learned arms now share identical PPO hyperparameters, the
dispersion across seeds is a property of the architecture. The centralized policy
is markedly the less stable of the two on the largest network: $\pm 51.4$
percentage points of offered load on Germany50 against $\pm 3.5$ for MARL at
$h{=}32$, and $\pm 5.90$ percentage points of packet loss against MARL's
$\pm 2.91$. Inspection of the individual seeds shows the mechanism: one
centralized seed collapses onto shortest-path selection, choosing the
cost-shortest of the $k=3$ candidates for almost every demand, which reproduces
OSPF and inflates the mean without inflating the training return. Across the
evaluation networks the centralized policy routes $62$--$95\%$ of demands on
exactly the OSPF path, against $47$--$80\%$ for MARL, so the collapse is a matter
of degree rather than an all-or-nothing failure. The decentralized policy, whose
per-node action space is re-entered at every hop and whose deviations are
therefore incremental rather than committing a whole path, is the more stable at
scale. On Abilene the ordering reverses and the centralized policy is the tighter
of the two ($\pm 0.82$ against $\pm 2.13$ points of loss), which is consistent
with it being the stronger arm there.

This reverses the expectation that a $50$-node network would be hardest for a
decentralized learner through multi-agent credit assignment, with each agent's
return depending on forty-nine others whose policies change concurrently. That
effect is real but is evidently not the binding constraint at this scale: MARL is
the stronger architecture on Germany50 under overload by $7.6$ to $9.3$ points of
loss. What survives of the scale argument is confined to the feasible regime,
where hop-by-hop construction produces routes that free headroom at a small but
consistent cost in loss, and where the centralized formulation's global view of a
whole path is the better tool.

The reported width, $h{=}32$, was selected on the training topologies
(Section~\ref{sec:setup-hyper}) and not on the evidence in this section. That
selection is corroborated here: $h{=}32$ is the stronger arm on Abilene and on
both GÉANT cells, and it carries roughly a third of the seed dispersion of
$h{=}64$ ($\pm 1.6$ against $\pm 3.0$ on GÉANT overload, $\pm 3.5$ against
$\pm 9.7$ on Germany50). The wider policy wins only the Germany50 overload cell,
and does so with three times the variance.

Against the centralized controller the two architectures split the six cells
evenly. MARL takes both GÉANT cells and Germany50 overload --- the three in which
the network is large or the demand heavy --- by $8.5$, $27.7$ and $7.6$ points
respectively. The centralized controller takes both Abilene cells and Germany50
feasible, the three in which OSPF is already close to optimal and the useful
deviations are few. The pattern is consistent with the mechanism argued above:
incremental per-hop diversification pays when there is slack to redistribute, and
whole-path selection pays when only a handful of targeted changes are worth
making.

That split is worth reading alongside the parameter counts of
Table~\ref{tab:hyper}. The reported decentralized policy carries $17{,}570$
trainable parameters against the centralized controller's $268{,}420$, a factor
of fifteen. Whatever advantage the decentralized formulation confers where it
wins, it is not bought with capacity.

\subsection{Where the Decentralized Method Fails: Abilene}
\label{sec:res-abilene}

Abilene is where the decentralized formulation loses, and it is the one network on
which the ranking of the two learned architectures inverts. MARL at $h{=}32$
records $0.97\%$ loss and $14.2$\,ms delay in the feasible regime against OSPF's
$0.17\%$ and $11.5$\,ms, and $15.41\%$ loss in overload against OSPF's $14.35\%$;
at $h{=}64$ it is worse still, $3.30\%$ and $24.8$\,ms. The centralized
controller, by contrast, matches OSPF's service in the feasible regime ($0.24\%$
loss, $13.8$\,ms) while holding the bottleneck $8.5$ points lower at $88.1\%$,
and takes the lowest loss in the cell under overload, $13.41\%$ against OSPF's
$14.35\%$ and ECMP's $14.30\%$.

So the negative result is specific rather than general: \emph{learned routing}
improves on OSPF at twelve nodes, but the hop-by-hop decentralized formulation
does not. Two properties of the network explain the asymmetry. First, Abilene is
the only evaluation instance with heterogeneous link capacities, fourteen links at
$9.92$\,Gbps and one at $2.48$\,Gbps; correctly costed, OSPF prices that link at
four and routes around it almost entirely, so the baseline is already close to
optimal and the margin available to any optimiser is thin. Second, with only
twelve nodes and $132$ demand pairs there is little scope for the incremental,
per-hop diversification that is MARL's advantage on larger networks, while its
per-hop detour penalty still accumulates. The centralized controller, choosing
whole cost-shortest paths, expresses exactly the small number of targeted
deviations that this network rewards.

This bounds the contribution in a useful way. The decentralized formulation's
value is concentrated in networks large enough, or loaded heavily enough, that
shortest-path routing leaves genuine slack on the table: it wins by $11.2$ points
of loss at twenty-two nodes and by $8.6$ at fifty, and loses by roughly one point
at twelve. The crossover lies below the scale of any of the three backbones
studied, which is a more informative statement than a uniform win would have been.

\subsection{The Reward Formulation}
\label{sec:res-reward}

The two architectures were initially trained under reward functions that differed
in a detail that proved consequential. Both charge the marginal increase in the
bottleneck, normalised by the episode's OSPF bottleneck so that networks at
different utilisations are comparable. They differed in the delay term: the
centralized environment charged $\beta$ per \emph{extra hop} relative to the
shortest candidate and normalised that penalty along with the congestion term,
whereas the decentralized environment charges a flat $\beta$ per \emph{detour
hop} and leaves it unnormalised.

Measured on the evaluation traffic, the centralized form made the shortest
candidate the myopically optimal choice for $95.2\%$ of GÉANT demands and
$97.2\%$ of Germany50 demands: for the large majority of individual demands no
alternative path reduces the bottleneck at all, so the congestion benefit is
exactly zero while the hop penalty is immediate and certain. The consequence was
a policy that reproduced OSPF on $90.3\%$ of GÉANT demands.

Retraining the centralized controller under the decentralized reward, with every
other setting held fixed, improves it in five of the six cells of
Table~\ref{tab:qos}. The largest gains are on Abilene, where feasible-regime loss
falls from $3.69\%$ to $0.24\%$ and delay from $37.8$ to $13.8$\,ms, and on
Germany50 feasible, where it frees a further $13.3$ points of headroom at
unchanged loss. It regresses only on Germany50 overload, $16.73\%$ to $18.17\%$.
The results reported throughout this chapter use the improved form; the original
is retained in \texttt{results/final\_ns3\_grid.json} as an ablation.

Two points follow. First, the baseline reported here is the stronger of the two
configurations tested, so the comparison is not flattered by an under-configured
opponent. Second, and more important for the interpretation of
Section~\ref{sec:res-stability}, the decentralized policy retains its advantage
under the identical reward function: $8.5$ points of loss on GÉANT overload,
$27.7$ points of utilisation on GÉANT feasible, and $7.6$ points of loss on
Germany50 overload. The architectural difference therefore does not reduce to the
reward asymmetry.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:res-limitations}

Six limitations qualify these results.

The policies are trained against an analytical link-utilisation surrogate rather
than ns-3, since packet-level simulation is too slow inside a training loop. The
agreement between objective and measurement in the overload regime shows the
surrogate is adequate there, but it does not guarantee untested regimes, and the
Germany50 feasible cell is precisely a case where the surrogate's ranking and the
packet-level ranking disagree.

The Germany50 OSPF baseline is sensitive to equal-cost tie-breaking. Germany50's
links are of uniform capacity, so weighting the metric does not change any path
cost; it changes only which of several equal-cost paths the shortest-path
computation returns. That choice alone moves OSPF's measured overload loss by
several percentage points, and it moves in the direction that flatters the
learned policies. The Germany50 margins should be read with this in mind.

The reward comparison of Section~\ref{sec:res-reward} was run for the
centralized architecture only. Whether the decentralized policy would likewise
improve under the centralized reward form was not tested, so the claim is that
the reported baseline is the stronger of two configurations, not that it is
optimal.

Three seeds is few, particularly given the dispersion documented in
Section~\ref{sec:res-stability}. The Germany50 feasible utilisation figures carry
seed standard deviations between $\pm 13$ and $\pm 20$ percentage points, and
differences between arms within that cell are not resolved by this evidence.

Abilene's two tables describe the same policies at different operating points, for
the reason given in Section~\ref{sec:setup-scaling}, and should not be read
across. Similarly, the absolute Germany50 figures correspond to the top $200$ of
$2450$ demand pairs; all methods see the identical flow set, so the comparison is
unbiased, but the magnitudes are those of that subset.

Finally, topologies are static and each simulation holds a single traffic matrix.
Link and node failures, convergence during a topology change, and adaptation to
traffic shifting \emph{within} an episode are not evaluated. The study
demonstrates generalisation across networks and across matrices, not adaptation
within one, and this remains the largest gap between the present work and a
deployable system.
