\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

% NOTE: every figure and table in this section is sourced from results/final_ns3_grid.json
% (378 ns-3 simulations, 0 failures, dirs results/ns3f_*). Earlier measurement batches used
% hop-count OSPF, capacity-blind candidate paths, or unmatched PPO hyperparameters and are
% not comparable; none of their numbers appear here.

A single policy, trained on seventeen SNDlib topologies and never shown the
evaluation networks, is tested zero-shot on Abilene, GÉANT and Germany50 driven
by real measured operator traffic. Under overload it reduces packet loss against
a correctly configured OSPF by $11.2$ percentage points on GÉANT and $18.4$ on
Germany50, and in the feasible regime it holds GÉANT's bottleneck at $66.7\%$
where OSPF leaves it at $97.3\%$, at no cost in loss or delay. On Abilene it
does not beat OSPF at all. The contribution is therefore a mechanism that
transfers to unseen networks and pays off where shortest-path routing runs out
of capacity, together with a clear statement of the topology on which it fails
and why.

The five methods, the matched $1.5$M-step budget, the shared optimiser settings,
and the regime stratification are as specified in
Section~\ref{sec:setup-methods}--\ref{sec:setup-regimes}. Because every learned
arm receives identical PPO hyperparameters, differences between them are
attributable to architecture rather than to tuning. Results are reported as mean
$\pm$ standard deviation \emph{across model seeds}, each seed contributing its
own mean over the retained matrices.

\subsection{Bottleneck Offered Load}
\label{sec:res-util}

Table~\ref{tab:offered-util} reports the training objective, the maximum offered
load of Equation~\eqref{eq:maxutil}, on the held-out networks. Recall from
Section~\ref{sec:setup-regimes} that this quantity is uncapped and may exceed
$100\%$, denoting demand no queueing discipline can serve; its consequence is
quantified in Section~\ref{sec:res-qos}.

Only four of the six network--regime cells exist at the evaluation loads. Under a
correctly weighted OSPF, Abilene has no overload regime at these loads: its
fourteen $9.92$\,Gbps links absorb the measured demand once the single
$2.48$\,Gbps link is correctly costed. Germany50 conversely has no feasible
regime, every measured matrix driving OSPF beyond capacity.

The decentralized policy at $h{=}32$ is the strongest arm in three of the four
cells and is never worse than the centralized one. On GÉANT it moves the
bottleneck from $145.5\%$ to $96.7\%$, restoring feasibility, while the
centralized policy at $147.4\%$ fails to improve on OSPF at all. On Germany50 the
$50$-node instance both learned architectures reduce a $252.1\%$ bottleneck, MARL
to $114.4\%$ and the centralized policy only to $185.3\%$.

Two observations qualify this. First, ECMP is a far stronger baseline than
shortest-path OSPF and must be cleared before any claim of improvement: it
reaches $106.3\%$ on GÉANT and $183.8\%$ on Germany50, so on GÉANT the
centralized policy is beaten not only by MARL but by a routing mechanism already
deployed in every operator network. Second, the centralized policy's Germany50
standard deviation of $\pm 33.9$ percentage points, against $\pm 3.5$ for MARL at
the same width and under identical hyperparameters, is an order of magnitude
larger than any other entry in the table. This is examined in
Section~\ref{sec:res-stability}.

\subsection{Packet-Level Quality of Service}
\label{sec:res-qos}

A reduction in a computed objective is not by itself a networking result: it is
possible to lower a bottleneck metric and deliver worse service by lengthening
paths until propagation delay dominates. To rule this out, the exact per-flow
routes produced by each policy were installed in ns-3 and driven with the same
held-out traffic under the configuration of Section~\ref{sec:setup-ns3}.
Table~\ref{tab:qos} reports the resulting measurements over $378$ simulations.

\paragraph{Overload.} On GÉANT, MARL at $h{=}32$ reduces loss from $14.13\%$ to
$2.92\%$, a factor of nearly five and a margin of $11.2$ percentage points, while
holding delay at $18.1$\,ms against OSPF's $21.4$\,ms. The delay result is the
more informative: the learned policy uses longer paths, so a naive expectation is
higher delay, and that it falls instead shows the dominant contribution under
OSPF was queueing at the saturated bottleneck rather than propagation. On
Germany50 the $h{=}64$ policy reduces loss from $27.29\%$ to $8.88\%$ and
simultaneously records the lowest delay in the cell, $15.1$\,ms against
$19.7$\,ms. The operational reading is direct: a link dropping one packet in
four, at which interactive and real-time traffic is unusable, is brought to a
loss rate an order of magnitude lower by changing route selection alone, with no
additional capacity provisioned.

The objective and the packet-level outcome agree throughout this regime.
Table~\ref{tab:offered-util} showed the centralized policy failing to restore
feasibility on GÉANT at $147.4\%$; Table~\ref{tab:qos} shows it correspondingly
retaining $13.29\%$ loss, essentially OSPF's. Policies that restore feasibility
deliver service, and policies that do not, do not.

\paragraph{Feasible.} The GÉANT feasible cell is the cleanest result in the
study: MARL at $h{=}32$ holds the bottleneck at $66.7\%$ against OSPF's $97.3\%$
while \emph{improving} both loss ($0.12\%$ against $0.16\%$) and delay
($8.3$\,ms against $9.5$\,ms). Thirty percentage points of headroom are freed at
no service cost, which is the margin an operator needs to absorb a link failure
or a traffic surge without re-engineering.

Germany50 behaves differently and the difference must not be averaged away. Both
MARL widths free substantially more headroom than OSPF, $70.8\%$ and $61.9\%$
against $95.6\%$, but they pay for it in loss: $1.02\%$ and $2.44\%$ against
OSPF's $0.03\%$. The centralized policy wins this cell outright, matching OSPF's
$0.03\%$ loss and $1.8$\,ms delay while still holding the bottleneck $20.8$
points lower at $74.8\%$. Where the traffic already fits, the whole-path
selection of the centralized formulation extracts headroom without disturbing
service, and the hop-by-hop MARL formulation does not.

\subsection{Seed Stability and the Architectural Comparison}
\label{sec:res-stability}

Because all four learned arms now share identical PPO hyperparameters, the
dispersion across seeds is a property of the architecture. The centralized policy
is markedly the less stable of the two: $\pm 33.9$ percentage points of offered
load on Germany50 against $\pm 3.5$ for MARL at $h{=}32$, and in the packet-level
measurements $\pm 4.97$ percentage points of loss and $\pm 37.1$\,ms of delay on
Abilene, against $\pm 1.02$ and $\pm 4.2$ for MARL. Inspection of the individual
seeds shows the mechanism: a subset of centralized seeds collapse onto
shortest-path selection, choosing the first of the $k=3$ candidates for nearly
every demand, which reproduces OSPF and inflates the mean without inflating the
training return. The decentralized policy, whose per-node action space is
re-entered at every hop, does not exhibit this failure.

This reverses the expectation that a $50$-node network would be hardest for a
decentralized learner through multi-agent credit assignment, with each agent's
return depending on forty-nine others whose policies change concurrently. That
effect is real but is evidently not the binding constraint at this scale: MARL is
the stronger architecture on Germany50 under overload by $6.1$ to $7.9$ points of
loss. What survives of the scale argument is confined to the feasible regime,
where hop-by-hop construction produces routes that free headroom at a small but
consistent cost in loss, and where the centralized formulation's global view of a
whole path is the better tool.

Between the two MARL widths, the narrower $h{=}32$ is preferable on the evidence.
It is the stronger arm on Abilene and on both GÉANT cells, and it carries roughly
a third of the seed dispersion of $h{=}64$ ($\pm 1.6$ against $\pm 3.0$ on GÉANT
overload, $\pm 3.5$ against $\pm 9.7$ on Germany50). The wider policy wins only
the Germany50 overload cell, and does so with three times the variance. The
capacity-matched width is therefore not the better configuration, and $h{=}32$ is
adopted as the reference decentralized policy.

\subsection{Where the Method Fails: Abilene}
\label{sec:res-abilene}

Abilene is a negative result and is reported as one. In the feasible regime every
learned method is worse than OSPF on both service metrics: loss of $0.97\%$
($h{=}32$), $3.30\%$ ($h{=}64$) and $3.69\%$ (centralized) against OSPF's
$0.17\%$, and delay of $14.2$, $24.8$ and $37.8$\,ms against $11.5$\,ms. In the
overload regime no learned method improves on OSPF's $14.35\%$ loss, and ECMP at
$14.30\%$ is nominally the best arm in the cell.

The explanation is structural rather than a matter of training. Abilene is the
only evaluation network with heterogeneous link capacities, fourteen links at
$9.92$\,Gbps and one at $2.48$\,Gbps. Correctly costed, OSPF assigns that link a
metric of four and routes around it almost entirely, and because the network is
small and well connected the resulting paths are close to optimal. There is
consequently very little for an optimiser to win, and the residual freedom the
learned policies exercise, taking a longer path to shave the bottleneck, costs
more in queueing and propagation than it recovers. The same property that made
Abilene appear to be the easiest network under a hop-count baseline, its single
slow link, makes it the hardest once the baseline is configured as it would be in
practice.

This is worth stating plainly because it bounds the contribution. The value of
learned routing here is concentrated in networks large enough, or loaded heavily
enough, that shortest-path routing leaves genuine slack on the table. At twelve
nodes with a well-configured metric, it does not.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:res-limitations}

Six limitations qualify these results.

The policies are trained against an analytical link-utilisation surrogate rather
than ns-3, since packet-level simulation is too slow inside a training loop. The
agreement between objective and measurement in the overload regime shows the
surrogate is adequate there, but it does not guarantee untested regimes, and the
Germany50 feasible cell is precisely a case where the surrogate's ranking and the
packet-level ranking disagree.

The Germany50 OSPF baseline is sensitive to equal-cost tie-breaking. Germany50's
links are of uniform capacity, so weighting the metric does not change any path
cost; it changes only which of several equal-cost paths the shortest-path
computation returns. That choice alone moves OSPF's measured overload loss by
several percentage points, and it moves in the direction that flatters the
learned policies. The Germany50 margins should be read with this in mind.

Three seeds is few, particularly given the dispersion documented in
Section~\ref{sec:res-stability}. The Germany50 feasible utilisation figures carry
seed standard deviations between $\pm 13$ and $\pm 20$ percentage points, and
differences between arms within that cell are not resolved by this evidence.

Abilene's two tables describe the same policies at different operating points, for
the reason given in Section~\ref{sec:setup-scaling}, and should not be read
across. Similarly, the absolute Germany50 figures correspond to the top $200$ of
$2450$ demand pairs; all methods see the identical flow set, so the comparison is
unbiased, but the magnitudes are those of that subset.

Finally, topologies are static and each simulation holds a single traffic matrix.
Link and node failures, convergence during a topology change, and adaptation to
traffic shifting \emph{within} an episode are not evaluated. The study
demonstrates generalisation across networks and across matrices, not adaptation
within one, and this remains the largest gap between the present work and a
deployable system.
